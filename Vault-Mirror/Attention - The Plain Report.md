## PART ONE — What attention actually is

**It is the part of your life you are present for.** Time passes either way. Attention is what you were doing while it passed.

**It is three things, not one.** People talk about attention as a single quantity you have more or less of. It isn't. Brain research separates it into three systems that fail independently:

1. **Being alert enough to notice anything.** This is what fails on a long drive or a night shift — you're awake, but you stop seeing.
2. **Choosing what to look at.** This is what fails when your phone buzzes and you're looking before you decided to.
3. **Staying on it when something is pulling.** This is what fails when you know exactly what you should be doing and you do the other thing.

They need three different fixes. Being tired needs movement or sleep. Being pulled needs the distraction physically removed. Being unable to start needs the task made smaller. Applying the wrong fix is why people conclude they're broken when they aren't.

**You can hold about four things at once.** That's everyone. It was long believed to be seven; better-controlled research revised it to roughly four.

**But you can change what counts as one thing.** A chess master doesn't see thirty-two pieces. He sees five or six familiar shapes. Same four slots, far more information in them. This is the single most useful fact in this report: *you never get more slots, you only get better at packing them.* Anything you've practiced until it runs by itself is a slot you got back permanently.

## PART TWO — Why it fails

Five distinct ways. They feel different and they need different responses.

**1. The long watch stops seeing.** On any monitoring task — driving, security, scanning — detection reliably drops the longer you're at it. This happens regardless of effort. Trying harder does not fix it. It also gets worse if there's too little happening, not just too much, so it isn't simple tiredness. The only real fix is ending the task or swapping in a fresh person. Short breaks inside the task don't help.

**2. The task you left behind follows you.** When you switch from one task to another, part of your mind stays on the first one, and it stays there — it does not fade after a minute. It makes you slower, less accurate, and shallower on the new task the entire time. It's worst when the first task was unfinished. Writing down where you left off genuinely helps, because the loop gets somewhere to sit.

**3. Coming back costs more than you think.** Field research in offices found it takes about 23 minutes on average to fully return to complex work after a digital interruption, usually by way of two other tasks first. Worth knowing: **44% of interruptions people experience are ones they cause themselves.**

Related and more alarming: the same researcher tracked how long people stay on one screen before switching. In 2004 it was two and a half minutes. By 2012, 75 seconds. Recently, about 47 seconds.

**4. Your mind wanders about half the time.** Large-scale sampling found people report thinking about something other than what they're doing roughly 47% of waking hours. This is normal. It is not a defect to be eliminated, and any system that requires you to eliminate it will fail daily.

**5. Deep focus can be broken before it starts.** Getting into genuinely absorbed work takes 15–25 minutes of *uninterrupted* engagement. An interruption during that window doesn't pause the clock — it resets it. A nine-second question costs the whole approach.

## PART THREE — The correction that matters most

The old idea was that willpower and focus are a fuel tank that drains. That theory — "ego depletion" — largely collapsed when researchers tried to reproduce it. The proposed fuel (glucose) turned out not to work that way, and two findings killed it: offering someone a reward restores their performance instantly, and simply changing what someone *believes* about willpower changes their results. Fuel tanks don't do that.

The better-supported idea: **the feeling of mental effort is a signal about what you're giving up.** Every minute on this task is a minute not on everything else. When the alternatives start looking better than continuing, you disengage — and that feels like exhaustion.

**But that only explains part of it, and this was the important correction in our conversation.** Two things it can't explain:

**Your capacity genuinely moves.** What you have available at 5am, 3pm and 11pm are different amounts, driven by sleep, time of day, how long you've been at it. Sometimes you really are low. That's a condition, not a character flaw, and it's worth saying plainly: *"I have no focus right now" is sometimes just true.*

**Some attention is taken, not spent.** A notification doesn't make an argument you could have resisted. Sudden movement, motion, a face, your own name, a red badge — these grab you before any decision happens. You cannot out-decide something that arrived before the decision.

And the reason this matters practically: **the reflex being exploited is a good reflex.** Sudden movement usually did matter. Novelty is genuinely rewarding to the brain — new information carries its own reward signal before you know whether it's worth anything. Deliver that on an unpredictable schedule and you have the most attention-holding arrangement known, which is exactly what a feed is.

So being caught by it is not weakness. It's being undefended. Those need different responses.

## PART FOUR — What restores attention

**The finding that surprised us most: feeling restored is not the same as being restored.**

A large review of short breaks (10 minutes or less) found they reliably improve how energetic you feel and reliably reduce how tired you feel — but showed **no reliable improvement in actual performance**. Performance only improved on easy tasks, and the longer the break, the bigger the gain. The authors noted directly that recovering from genuinely demanding work needs more than ten minutes.

Practical translation: after hard work, a six-minute break makes you feel ready and does not make you ready. That's worth knowing before you trust the feeling.

**What works, roughly in order of how well established it is:**

**Sleep.** The largest single thing you control. Nothing else on this list compensates for missing it. Someone sleeping five hours isn't a discipline problem, they're an under-resourced system.

**Exercise.** Well supported, both a single session and long term. Notably, short moderate-to-hard exercise significantly improves focus in people who are sleep-deprived — making it a better emergency fix than more coffee. Harder exercise helps alertness most; easier exercise helps memory most.

**Removing the distraction rather than resisting it.** Distance beats willpower and doesn't wear out over the day.

**Finishing things, or writing down where you stopped.** This is the direct fix for problem #2 above and it's one of the best-supported items here.

**A short breathing practice.** A well-run 2023 trial compared five minutes of daily breathwork against five minutes of meditation. The one that won — "cyclic sighing," two inhales through the nose then a long slow exhale through the mouth — beat meditation on mood, with effects measurable after a single five-minute session. Only one strong study so far, so treat it as promising rather than settled. Costs almost nothing to try.

**Naps, chosen deliberately.** Ten minutes gives immediate alertness with no grogginess and lasts up to about 2.5 hours. Twenty to twenty-six minutes gives a bigger performance gain but you wake up slightly foggy. Past thirty minutes you risk deep sleep and real grogginess. So: ten if you need to be sharp immediately, twenty-six if you have buffer.

**Genuinely switching off in the evening.** Research on recovery finds that failing to mentally disconnect from work damages sleep quality, which then damages the next day. Disconnecting requires closed loops — which is why writing tomorrow's priority down before bed does more than it looks like it does.

**And one counterintuitive one:** hard activity in an *unrelated* area counts as recovery, not extra load. Training, a build project, learning a language — these restore you from your main work while being demanding themselves. Rest doesn't have to mean doing nothing.

## PART FIVE — What actually builds it

Most things sold as brain training don't work. Puzzle games and memory apps reliably make you better at *that game* and the evidence that this transfers to real life is weak and contested. Don't spend money there.

**What does have real evidence:**

**Meditation-style attention training — about 12 minutes a day.** The strongest evidence here comes from military research: soldiers and Marines in high-stress pre-deployment periods. Two findings stand out. First, **without any training, attention and memory measurably decline** under that kind of stress — so just holding steady is already a win. Second, those who practiced at least 12 minutes a day didn't merely hold steady, they **improved**. More practice, more benefit.

The working dose is 12–15 minutes, 3–4 days a week.

**The most important coaching point, and the one people get wrong:** the repetition is *not* staying focused. The repetition is **noticing you drifted and coming back**. Your mind will leave. That's the machine working normally. Each time you notice and return, that's one rep. A session where you drifted forty times and returned forty times is a good session. A session where you never drifted is just a shorter one.

**Two other findings from that research worth keeping:** the version emphasizing actual practice beat the version emphasizing teaching people *about* stress. And instructors who knew the population outperformed meditation experts who didn't. Doing beats reading, and familiar language beats expert language.

**Practicing sequences until they're automatic.** This is the "bigger chunks" point from Part One. Every routine you drill until it runs by itself permanently frees capacity. It's the only reliable way to get more out of a fixed limit.

## PART SIX — What we found in the existing system

We audited fourteen standing assumptions in the doctrine against the research. **Eight held up, three held up and turned out better-founded than written, three needed correcting.** No serious failures. Where corrections were needed, they were to the *explanation*, not the conclusion — the practices were right, the reasons were sometimes wrong.

**The three biggest things found:**

**The hourly check-in was doing something nobody had named.** The rule that every hour opens with an observation and closes with a log is, mechanically, the exact documented fix for problem #2 — the task that follows you. It's the best-evidenced single practice in the whole system, and it was written for a different reason.

**The hourly bell is fighting fire with fire.** Distractions capture attention by being sudden and salient. The hourly mark is a sudden salient event *you scheduled*, aimed at pulling you back. That's the right counter, and the research supports it: an external cue is how captured attention gets recovered. You cannot out-discipline being grabbed. You can out-schedule it.

**The drill practice was undervalued.** It had been filed as memory work. It's actually the only known way to increase effective capacity. It should be treated as building something, not maintaining something.

**One thing found by being wrong twice:** the doctrine is much more developed than it looks from any single entry point, because material lives under domain names rather than resource names. The knowledge doctrine, for instance, is substantial — a full seven-stage cycle, a source list, report classes — but none of it is titled "Intel," so searching for the label finds nothing. **The gap is a missing index, not missing content.**

## PART SEVEN — The honesty section

What's solid, what's shaky, what we invented.

**Solid.** The three attention systems. The ~4 item limit. Sleep and exercise. The 15–25 minute ramp into deep focus and that interruptions reset it. Attention residue. That short breaks restore feeling more than function. The ~47% mind-wandering figure. Attention training working at ~12 minutes a day.

**Reasonable but not settled.** The breathing practice — one good trial, needs replication. Nap numbers — consistent findings, but the well-known figures come from a single 1995 pilot study. Psychological detachment — mostly field studies rather than experiments.

**Popular but thinner than its reputation.** Nature restoring attention — widely cited, and a systematic review noted the evidence base is weaker than the citation count implies. No harm using it. Don't build on it.

**Commonly repeated and not reliable.** The claim that having your phone in the room drains you even unused — the famous study failed to replicate. The claim that heavy multitaskers have damaged attention — the original finding hasn't held up cleanly and the effect, if real, is small. The anti-multitasking position is still correct; it just needs to rest on switch cost and residue instead.

**Not a real thing.** "Dopamine detox" as brain chemistry. You cannot detox from a neurotransmitter your brain requires. Taking a break from high-stimulation media is a fine practice — it works through habit and environment, not a chemical reset — and the extreme versions carry real downside.

**Contested.** Caffeine timing rules. Caffeine works. The popular "wait 90 minutes after waking" rule is not established; one military study found the opposite. Individual variation is large.

**What we constructed rather than found.** The interval grades — 20/10, 24/6, 48/12, and the deep-work block — are a *designed ladder*, not a set of validated intervals. The individual anchor points draw on real findings (the ~20–25 minute attention curve, the flow entry ramp, the "more than ten minutes" rest requirement). The specific ratios are ours. They are reasonable starting hypotheses. They are not established best practice, and nobody should present them as such.

## A NOTE ON THE 2.5 HOUR BLOCK

This was asked directly and deserves a direct answer: **2.5 hours of work followed by 30 minutes of rest is not established best practice, and the number came from the conversation rather than from the research.**

Here's what is actually supported:

- Deep focus takes **15–25 minutes to enter**, and interruption resets that. Solid.
- Once entered, it **can** sustain for hours if the task stays well-matched to skill and physical needs are met. Solid.
- Recovery from demanding work needs **more than ten minutes**, and longer breaks return more. Solid.

Here's what isn't:

- **There is no validated optimal work interval.** Not 25 minutes, not 52, not 90, not 150. The commonly cited figures come from productivity-app datasets and popular writing, not controlled trials.
- **2.5 hours has one specific tension against it.** The body runs a roughly 90–120 minute cycle of rising and falling alertness. If that's right, a natural dip arrives *before* 2.5 hours is up. That argues for **90–120 minutes as the default deep block**, with 2.5 hours as an upper bound for unusually good sessions rather than the standard.

**Recommendation:** move the default to 90–120 minutes, keep 2.5 hours as the exception, and keep the 30-minute rest — that part is well supported for demanding work. Then log it for a few weeks and find the real number, which will be personal.

The shape is right: **long uninterrupted block, protected start, substantial rest after.** The specific numbers are a starting position, not a finding.
